Understanding Mass Spectrometry

A mass spectrometer weighs molecules precisely enough to tell nearly identical substances apart. Here is why that matters, and how it works, explored with a simulator you drive yourself.

Why this matters

You have almost certainly benefited from a mass spectrometer today without knowing it. It is one of the quiet workhorses of modern science, and its job is deceptively simple. It weighs molecules, one kind at a time, precisely enough to tell nearly identical substances apart.

That single ability turns out to matter almost everywhere. When regulators check that your food, your medicine, and your supplements are free of contaminants, heavy metals, and substances that are not supposed to be there, a mass spectrometer is usually what makes the call. When a forensic lab needs to identify an illicit drug in a sample, or prove exactly which one it was, this is the instrument that answers. Biologists use it to watch metabolism as it happens, measuring how living things build up and break down the molecules that keep them alive. And environmental scientists depend on it to find pollution at concentrations far too small to see, taste, or smell, tracing contaminants through water, soil, and air.

Different fields, same trick underneath. You figure out what something is by weighing its pieces very, very carefully. Once you can do that, you can answer questions that used to be impossible.

And the best way to understand how it works is to run one. You already can. Open the Smart Turtle simulator in a new tab and keep it beside this page. Every step below is something for you to try yourself. Do the clicking, watch what moves, and the ideas will stick better than any diagram could. Leave each result on the graph as you go, since later steps are easier to see when your earlier ones are still there to compare against.

Six things to try yourself

  1. 1

    A molecule is a family of peaks

    Start simple. The formula box already says C6H12O6, which is glucose. Press Simulate.

    Notice you did not get one clean line. You got a little cluster of peaks. That cluster is called the isotope envelope, and it is there because atoms come in slightly heavier and lighter versions called isotopes. Most carbon atoms weigh the same, but a small fraction weigh a touch more, and every real sample is a mix. The tallest peak is the most common version of the molecule. The shorter peaks just to its right are the copies that happened to catch one or two of those heavier atoms. Every molecule you ever measure shows up as a family like this, not a single spike.

  2. 2

    The bottom axis is mass divided by charge

    Open the Advanced settings and find the Adduct menu. It starts on +H. Switch it to +2H and Simulate again.

    The whole pattern jumps to a new place, and the peaks bunch closer together. This is the most misunderstood thing about mass spectrometry, so it is worth slowing down for. The number along the bottom is not the molecule's weight by itself. It is the weight divided by the charge the molecule is carrying. Give a molecule two charges instead of one and you are dividing by twice as much, so it lands at roughly half its former position, and the gaps between its peaks shrink by half too. Now switch the adduct to +Na and watch it move somewhere else again, because this time you changed the weight by handing the molecule a heavier passenger. It is the same molecule in every run. What the axis reports is always mass and charge together, never one without the other.

  3. 3

    How finely can the instrument see?

    Still in Advanced, find Resolving power and slide it down low, then Simulate.

    Your crisp separate peaks smear into blobs. Every real instrument has a limit on how finely it can tell two nearby weights apart, and that limit is what you just lowered. Now go back to the Basic settings and toggle between Show profile data and Show centroided data. Profile is the raw, rounded signal the machine actually records. Centroid is the cleaned-up stick version scientists usually put in figures. Same measurement, two ways of drawing it. Knowing they are the same thing keeps you from being confused when you see both in the wild.

  4. 4

    The signal is never perfectly clean

    In Advanced, raise Baseline noise and Simulate.

    Fuzz creeps along the bottom of the graph, and here is the part to watch: the small peaks disappear into it first. The big peaks shrug the noise off. The little ones get swallowed. That is a general rule of measurement, not a quirk of this tool. It also sets up the rest of the lesson, because the next two steps live entirely in those small right-hand peaks. Anything happening out there is real only if it stands above the fuzz. Turn the noise back down before you continue.

  5. 5

    Labeling changes the recipe

    Now the interesting part. Open the Isotope enrichment tab and raise the Deuterium percentage, then Simulate. Keep your earlier plain run on the graph so you can see both.

    The whole envelope walks to the right and grows heavier, right next to the unlabeled version. Deuterium is just hydrogen that weighs more. If you feed a living thing water made with heavy hydrogen, every molecule it builds while the water is around comes out slightly heavier. That rightward shift is the fingerprint of freshly made material. Scientists use exactly this to tell brand-new molecules apart from old ones, without ever disturbing the organism.

  6. 6

    How far can it shift?

    Last step, and the payoff. Keep the deuterium high. Now find Labelable hydrogens, the box marked nL, and set it to a small number. Simulate.

    The shift shrinks and then refuses to grow, no matter how much heavy hydrogen is available. That is the whole idea of nL. A molecule can only get so heavy, because only certain spots in its structure are able to pick up a heavy atom in the first place. That count of eligible spots is nL, and it is set by how the molecule is built. Two different molecules given the very same heavy water can shift by different amounts, purely because one has more labelable spots than the other.

    One last thing to try, because it is the honest lesson hiding in these two knobs. A smaller shift than you expected can mean two completely different things: fewer labelable spots, or simply less heavy hydrogen around. You just moved both sliders. In a real experiment, telling those two causes apart is a real part of the job, and now you have felt why.

That is mass spectrometry from the bottom up: a family of peaks, placed by weight and charge, blurred by the instrument, threatened by noise, and shifted by labeling exactly as far as the molecule's own structure allows.

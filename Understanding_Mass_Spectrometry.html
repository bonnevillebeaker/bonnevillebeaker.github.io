Understanding Mass Spectrometry

How a mass spectrometer turns ions into a spectrum—and how to read what the spectrum is telling you.

01

The central idea

What is mass spectrometry?

Mass spectrometry is an analytical technique that creates gas-phase ions and separates or detects them according to their mass-to-charge ratio, written m/z.

A mass spectrometer does not simply “weigh a molecule.” First, the sample must be converted into ions. Those ions are then guided through an analyzer, where their behavior depends on m/z. A detector records how much signal arrives at each m/z value, producing a mass spectrum.

1 Introduce sample Liquid, gas, solid extract, or separated chromatographic peak
→
2 Create ions Examples: electrospray, electron ionization, MALDI
→
3 Separate by m/z Examples: quadrupole, TOF, Orbitrap, ion trap
→
4 Detect ions The instrument converts ion arrival into an electrical signal
Important distinction: mass spectrometry measures ions. Neutral molecules must gain or lose charge—or be converted into charged fragments—before the mass analyzer can detect them.

02

Reading the output

What do the axes mean?

x

Horizontal axis: m/z

m is the ion’s mass and z is the magnitude of its charge. A singly charged ion usually appears near its ionic mass. A doubly charged ion appears at roughly half that value.

y

Vertical axis: intensity

Intensity represents the detector signal associated with ions at that m/z. Spectra are often normalized so the strongest peak—the base peak—is shown as 100%.

Intensity is not automatically concentration. Different molecules ionize, transmit, fragment, and reach the detector with different efficiencies. Quantitative measurements normally require calibration, controls, or internal standards.
Smart Turtle exercise 2

Read the x- and y-axes

Use the glucose spectrum from Exercise 1 to connect each plotted feature to the information reported in the text panel.

  1. Turn on Show Centroided Data and simulate glucose again.
  2. Find the tallest peak. Its intensity is normalized to approximately 100%; this is the base peak.
  3. Match one centroid stick to its line in the text output. Compare the listed m/z and normalized intensity with the graph.
  4. Ask yourself whether a taller peak necessarily means that the original sample contained a proportionally larger concentration of that molecule.
Watch for: The horizontal position reports m/z; the vertical height reports relative detector response. Intensity is not automatically concentration.

Why this works: Ionization and transmission efficiencies can differ substantially between compounds.

03

Separating nearby signals

Resolution and resolving power

Resolution describes how close two ion signals can be while still being distinguished. Instruments commonly report resolving power:

R=mΔm

At m/z 500, a resolving power of 10,000 corresponds to a peak width of approximately 0.05 Da when Δm is measured at full width at half maximum. Higher resolving power produces narrower peaks and makes nearby ions easier to distinguish.

Smart Turtle exercise 3

Change the resolving power

Resolving power controls peak width and determines whether nearby exact-mass signals blend together or separate.

  1. Click Reset graph, enter caffeine as C8H10N4O2, and choose +H⁺.
  2. In Advanced settings, turn on Fix graph window and set the m/z range from M0 to 1.2. Turn on Show profile data, set resolving power to 2,000, and simulate.
  3. Without resetting, change the resolving power to 100,000 and simulate again. The second trace will be overlaid in a new color.
  4. Compare the widths of the two traces. Then inspect the M+1 region for exact-mass signals that become distinguishable at higher resolving power.
Watch for: Higher resolving power produces narrower profile peaks. Narrower peaks reveal nearby isotope fine structure that was hidden inside the broad low-resolution envelope.

Why this works: Because R = m/Δm, increasing R decreases the peak width Δm at a fixed m/z.

Two ways to represent a peak

Profile data and centroided data

Smart Turtle exercise 4

Compare profile and centroided data

Smart Turtle can display the sampled peak shape, centroid sticks, or both at once.

  1. Reset the graph and enter C6H12O6 with the +H⁺ adduct.
  2. Select both Show Profile Data and Show Centroided Data, then simulate.
  3. Notice that each centroid stick summarizes the center and intensity of a broader profile peak.
  4. Turn off profile data and simulate again. Compare the compact peak list with the information that was visible in the profile shape.

Why this works: A centroid is an estimated peak center—not an infinitely narrow physical signal.

Open as an SME pane Open in a new tab

Profile data

Profile data preserve the sampled shape of each peak across many adjacent m/z points. They contain information about width, shape, noise, and shoulders—but require more storage.

Centroided data

Centroiding summarizes each detected peak as a single m/z value and an intensity. The result is compact and convenient for peak lists, database searching, and downstream analysis.

A centroid is an estimate of a peak’s center, not a claim that the original signal was infinitely narrow. Poor peak detection or overlapping peaks can produce incorrect centroids.
05

Why the observed m/z differs from neutral mass

Adducts and charge

What is an adduct?

An adduct ion forms when a molecule is detected together with an added charged species or after the molecule loses a charged species. Common electrospray examples include protonated [M+H]+, sodiated [M+Na]+, and deprotonated [M−H]− ions.

The same neutral compound can therefore produce several peaks with different m/z values. Recognizing adducts prevents you from mistaking one molecule for several unrelated molecules.

General relationship
m/z ≈ (M + added mass − lost mass) / |z|
Exact calculations use the appropriate ionic masses and sign conventions.
Smart Turtle exercise 5A

Give the same molecule different adducts

An adduct changes the ion composition and therefore changes where the same neutral molecule appears on the m/z axis.

  1. Reset the graph, enter C6H12O6, turn on centroided data, and turn off profile data.
  2. Select +H⁺ and simulate.
  3. Without resetting, select +Na⁺ and simulate again.
  4. Compare the two monoisotopic peaks and the text output. Both signals originated from glucose, but one ion carries a proton and the other carries sodium.
Watch for: The sodium adduct appears at a higher m/z than the protonated ion. Adduct recognition prevents one compound from being mistaken for several unrelated compounds.

Smart Turtle exercise 5B

See how charge changes m/z and isotope spacing

Mass is divided by the magnitude of the charge. The spacing between isotope peaks is divided by charge as well.

  1. Reset the graph and enter C20H32O2. Keep centroided data on.
  2. Select +H⁺, simulate, and note the monoisotopic m/z and spacing between neighboring neutromers.
  3. Without resetting, select +2H²⁺ and simulate.
  4. Compare the two traces. The doubly charged ion appears near half the m/z, and neighboring one-neutron groups are separated by roughly 0.5 m/z instead of roughly 1.0.
Watch for: Both the ion mass and each isotope mass difference are divided by |z|.

Why this works: Multiply charged ions allow large molecules to appear within a practical analyzer range.

06

Following atoms through chemistry and biology

How isotope labeling changes the isotope distribution

Every element has naturally occurring isotopes. A molecule therefore appears as an isotope distribution: a group of isotopologues that contain different combinations of light and heavy isotopes.

Isotope labeling deliberately increases the abundance of a heavy isotope—such as 2H, 13C, or 15N. As label is incorporated, signal moves away from the unlabeled M0 peak and into heavier M+1, M+2, … peaks. The envelope may shift, broaden, or develop a new labeled population depending on the experiment.

Reading isotope fine structure

What is a neutromer?

A neutromer is the portion of an ion’s isotope distribution that has the same number of additional neutrons relative to the monoisotopic M0 form. The M+1 neutromer, for example, contains every molecular variant that has gained one neutron—even when that neutron is carried by a different isotope.

Those variants are extremely close in mass, but they are not perfectly identical. A nucleus weighs slightly less than the separate protons and neutrons used to build it because some mass is converted into nuclear binding energy. Different isotopes have different binding energies, so “adding one neutron” does not always add exactly 1 Da. This small departure from an integer mass is called the mass defect.

At modest resolving power, the exact-mass peaks within a neutromer blend together into one M+1, M+2, or M+3 peak. At high resolving power, the instrument can separate this isotopic fine structure.

All add one neutron Exact mass shift
14N → 15N+0.997035 Da
12C → 13C+1.003355 Da
1H → 2H+1.006277 Da
Values shown for neutral isotope substitutions. In an ion, the observed spacing on the m/z axis is divided by the charge magnitude |z|.
In Smart Turtle: selecting “Collapse centroid and text into neutromers” adds together all fine-structure signals with the same additional-neutron count and reports one probability-weighted centroid for each M group.
Smart Turtle exercise 6

Enrich a heavy isotope

Stable-isotope enrichment redistributes probability from the unlabeled molecule into heavier isotopologues.

  1. Reset the graph and enter C6H12O6 with +H⁺. Turn on profile and centroided data.
  2. Set Labelable Hydrogens (nₗ) to 12. Leave deuterium enrichment blank and simulate the natural-abundance spectrum.
  3. Enter 2 for % deuterium and simulate again without resetting.
  4. Increase deuterium to 10 and simulate once more.
  5. Repeat the comparison with nₗ set to 6. This limits the number of hydrogen positions allowed to draw from the enriched isotope pool.
Watch for: As enrichment rises, M0 loses relative intensity and signal moves into heavier neutromers. Increasing nₗ allows a broader range of labeled molecules.

Why this works: The isotope distribution records probabilities across many molecules; it does not mean that one molecule physically moves from peak to peak.

Smart Turtle exercise 7

Resolve and collapse neutromers

A neutromer groups isotope signals with the same additional-neutron count, even though their exact masses can differ slightly.

  1. Reset the graph and enter caffeine, C8H10N4O2, with +H⁺. Set resolving power to 100,000, turn on Fix graph window, and set the m/z range from M0 to 1.2.
  2. Turn on profile and centroided data, leave Collapse centroid and text into neutromers off, and simulate.
  3. Inspect the M+1 region and the text output. Signals such as a 13C substitution, a 15N substitution, or a 2H substitution all add one neutron but have slightly different exact masses.
  4. Turn on Collapse centroid and text into neutromers and simulate again. Smart Turtle now sums the fine-structure probabilities into one M+1 group and reports a probability-weighted centroid.
Watch for: The fine-structure peaks share an added-neutron count but differ slightly because isotope-specific nuclear binding energies produce different mass defects.

Why this works: At lower resolving power these signals merge physically; the collapse option gives you the corresponding grouped representation deliberately.

07

Why scientists use it

What is mass spectrometry used for?

?

Identify compounds

Combine accurate mass, isotope pattern, retention time, and fragmentation to narrow or confirm molecular identity.

#

Measure amount

Quantify drugs, metabolites, pollutants, peptides, and other analytes using standards and calibrated responses.

✂

Study structure

Fragment selected ions in tandem mass spectrometry to reveal substructures, sequences, and bond connectivity.

⌁

Profile complex systems

Measure thousands of proteins, lipids, metabolites, or environmental chemicals in a single study.

↻

Trace metabolism

Use stable isotopes to determine where atoms came from and how quickly molecules are synthesized or replaced.

✓

Test quality and safety

Check product purity, confirm manufacturing consistency, and detect contaminants or prohibited substances.

Key ideas to remember

1

A mass spectrometer detects ions, not neutral molecules.

2

The horizontal axis is m/z; the vertical axis is detector intensity.

3

Higher resolving power means narrower peaks and better separation of nearby ions.

4

Centroiding converts a sampled peak shape into a compact m/z–intensity pair.

5

Adducts and charge determine where a molecule appears on the m/z axis.

6

Isotope labeling redistributes signal into heavier isotopologues, making atom movement measurable.

7

A neutromer groups isotope signals with the same added-neutron count; nuclear binding energy gives its members slightly different exact masses.
